# Internal pre-submission review

Two independent reviews written in the role of journal referees (machining dynamics; nonlinear and robust control), produced by AI agents on the first complete draft (61 preprint pages), with an editorial triage in `review_triage.json`. They are not reviews by the journal. Section, equation and figure numbers refer to that draft.

## Reviewer A (machining dynamics): major revision

**Summary.** The manuscript takes the saturation-aware, speed-scheduled direct velocity feedback (DVF) of Ozsoy, Sims & Ozturk (2026) for a single-mode flexible workpiece driven by a proof-mass actuator, rebuilds the model, and asks what the 9 V command clip does to the controlled half-immersion milling cut. It argues that the published authority test, as reproduced with an assumed step entry from rest, is set by the entry transient. It replaces that test with a closed-form condition on the steady tooth-periodic forced command up to 0.9 a_lim, followed by a time-domain check of the clipped step entry. A full-surface-memory simulator then shows that the clipped loop is bistable: a step entry chatters below 0.97 a_lim at 157 of 251 speeds while a face entry reaches a_lim. At four speeds near the limit, velocity kicks of 2.4–10.8 % of a tooth's mean cutting impulse trigger chatter, and a dual-input describing function ties the basin size near the limit to the voltage margin V_max − b_max that the forced command leaves free. Ellipsoidal inner estimates of the basin are certified by sector-condition LMIs whose dead zone is centred on the forced command. These are proved on Chebyshev-collocation models of the averaged (C2) and time-periodic (C3) dynamics, checked independently of the solver, and found to be 1.5–28 times below the simulated critical kicks and never violated in simulation. Finally, a published delayed-difference (comb) term that vanishes on the forced orbit is analysed under the same clip and authority rule. It raises the linear limit by a median factor of 1.78 (g2 = 2000 V s/m) and widens the simulated basin where the DVF certificate almost vanishes, but it is fragile to loop delay with assumed higher modes, to delay mismatch and to runout. The whole study is computational.

**Strengths**

- The numerical work is unusually careful and transparent: an independent semi-discretisation check of the ZOA limit, a convergence study against an extrapolated exact-ZOH reference, and the identification of the half-step force delay of the usual held-force integration scheme (up to 9 % error in limits), a point useful to the whole community. The full-surface-memory chip model is validated against a 600-pass brute force, the bias of the chatter classifier is quantified, and every LMI candidate passes eigenvalue and Cholesky checks done independently of the solver.
- The central physical message is clear and relevant to practice. With a saturating actuator, the authority consumed by the forced tooth-periodic vibration, not the linear stability limit, decides how large a disturbance the controlled cut can absorb, and the entry strategy is part of the design. Figures 3–5 show this convincingly.
- The dual-input describing-function estimate, Eq. (23), which tends to (V_max − b_max)/(g p) at the limit, is an elegant and cheap explanation of the basin size near the limit. It agrees with the simulations within 0.88–1.13 at 0.95–0.98 a_lim.
- Centring the generalised sector condition on the forced command b(t) is a simple and appropriate way to bring the authority spent on the forced orbit into a region-of-attraction certificate. The periodic extension (piecewise-linear W(t), the convex-hull argument on (s, s^2), Petersen merging of the residual vertices) appears correct. The certificates are cross-validated against an independent simulator, including full-state off-axis perturbations.
- Section 6 is built on a deliberately fair baseline: the same authority rule, the gain cap lifted where DVF sits on it, and same-g1 designs that separate added damping from freed authority. Negative results are reported, namely no step-entry gain at high speed, the fragility to loop delay, delay mismatch and runout, and certificate-optimal gains that are not basin-optimal.
- Limitations are stated candidly: computational study, certificates on collocation models only, no decay rate, classifier bias. The novelty statement is modest, prior work is cited extensively, Table 1 separates published, derived and chosen parameters, and the code and stored results are to be released.

**Major comments**

- **M1** (Whole manuscript, especially Sections 3–6 (61 preprint pages; Sections 1–8 about 14,500 words, appendices about 2,000; Sections 4, 5 and 6 about 2,600, 3,300 and 3,500 words)). The manuscript reads as four papers in one: a critique and reformulation of the published authority test, a bistability map, LMI certificates on two models, and a delayed-term study. In places it reads like a laboratory notebook. Almost every sentence carries several numbers, often with 3–6 significant figures (e.g. 0.99999–1.0011, C3/C2 median 1.064, 6×10^-6). Many statements recur. The D1 classifier bias (0.05–1.5 %, 4.4 %) appears in Sections 4.1, 4.8 and 7 and in Appendix A. 'No experiment' appears in Sections 1, 3.4, 4.8, 5.7, 6.8 and 7. 'Theorems about the collocation model, not the delay equation' appears in Sections 5.1, 5.7, 6.3, 6.8 and 8. Each section ends with its own limitations list. For a machining-dynamics reader the main argument is hard to follow, and important caveats are buried in long paragraphs.  
  *Suggestion:* Either (a) split the work into two papers: (i) clipped DVF, covering the authority condition, the bistability and the certificates; (ii) authority-preserving delayed feedback under saturation. Or (b) shorten to about 9,000–10,000 words. Condense Section 3.1 to one paragraph and move the details to an appendix. Move the C3 machinery to an appendix or supplementary file: the vertex and Petersen details, the decomposition and segment-convergence paragraphs of Section 5.6, the a_e/D = 0.1 paragraph, and the straight-tooth counterexample with Table 5. Do the same with the P-term results of Section 6. Collect all limitations in one subsection of Section 7. Report secondary numbers in tables with 2–3 significant figures and state each key number once. Add a nomenclature table for the internal labels (C2, C3, D1, step/face entry, kick, authority, map design, same-g1 design).
- **M2** (Section 1 (contributions), Section 4.3 (mechanism), Section 4.6 (describing function), Section 5.1). A stabilising feedback with a bounded input cannot globally stabilise an exponentially unstable plant, so the closed loop has a bounded basin. This is textbook saturated-control theory (bounded null-controllable region; Hu & Lin, cited). Here the uncontrolled cut is unstable at every depth above a_lim(0). A bounded basin above a_lim(0), and none below it, is therefore expected rather than discovered, and the paper's own finding that the band edge is at least 1.08 a_lim(0) is consistent with it. For inertial actuators the 'stable at small, unstable at large amplitude' behaviour is established (Baumann & Elliott). Saturation-induced loss of stability in active vibration control of milling was reported, and modelled in the frequency domain, by Ozsoy et al. (2025). The overlap with that model is not specified. If it already contains a describing function of the clip that includes the forced component, Section 4.6 partly re-derives it. Linear systems with saturation and persistent disturbances, where the disturbance uses up part of the input range, have also been analysed with invariant-set tools (e.g. Hu, Lin & Chen, Automatica 38 (2002) 351–359). That is the general setting of the 'moving dead zone' argument.  
  *Suggestion:* State in Sections 1 and 4 that a bounded basin above a_lim(0) follows from saturated-control theory, and present the contribution as its quantification for regenerative milling: entry dependence, basin versus depth and speed, the role of b_max, and certificates that include the forced command. Describe precisely what the model of Ozsoy et al. (2025) contains (forced component? describing function? threshold amplitude?) and how Eqs. (22)–(23) go beyond it. Position the moving-dead-zone sector condition against the saturation-plus-disturbance literature.
- **M3** (Sections 2.5, 4 and 7; Figs. 9 and 11 of Ozsoy et al. [19]). Ozsoy et al. [19] report cutting tests with and without control on this set-up. These are the only measurements available, and the manuscript does not mention them. According to the authors' own repository, the linear model at the published gains classifies 17 of the 19 distinct published cuts correctly. The two failures are controlled cuts that chattered well below the predicted limit. The first is at 1300 rpm and 10 mm (g ≈ 1158 V s/m, a_lim = 12.5 mm, i.e. 0.80 a_lim); its measured chatter frequency of 149.5 Hz is close to the predicted 149.0 Hz. The second is at 2800 rpm and 5.5 mm (g ≈ 680 V s/m, 0.76 a_lim). These are exactly the events this paper is about. I ran the supplied simulator with the clip, full surface memory and the published gains. The forced command is about 3.5 V and 3.0 V (5.5–6 V of margin), and step and face entries settle at both points. At 1300 rpm the critical kick is 25–26 mm/s at phases 0 and 8 of 16, about 23 % of the tooth impulse of Eq. (21) and more than twice the 2.4–10.8 % quoted near the limit. At 2800 rpm no kick up to 3 m/s causes chatter at phases 0 and 8. If this is confirmed, the clip does not explain the 2800 rpm cut, and the model over-predicts the controlled limit there by at least about 25 %. That error is larger than most effects quantified in Sections 3–6. (Reviewer's check: scratchpad/review/reviewerA/kick_published.py, outputs kick_1300.txt and kick_2800.txt.)  
  *Suggestion:* Add a short validation subsection, e.g. in Section 2.5, comparing the model with all published test points of [19] at the published gains. Discuss the two chattering cuts: forced-command margin, position relative to the predicted band, and critical kick at all 16 phases. Then calibrate the quantitative claims (3 % thresholds, 2.4–10.8 %, the factor 1.78) against the demonstrated accuracy of the model, including the 0.5–4 Hz chatter-frequency discrepancies of the labelled cuts.
- **M4** (Section 3.1, Section 3.4 (Table 2), Fig. 2a, abstract (first finding), conclusion 1). The finding that the published test is 'ill-posed' and set by the entry transient rests on an assumed start: a step entry from rest with zero history. By the authors' own account, that start reproduces the published gains only at 2320–3000 rpm. In the repository the reproduced map is 0.74 times the published gains at 500–1150 rpm, 0.88 times at 1160–1490 rpm and 1.2–1.9 times at 2060–2310 rpm. The isolated pockets that fragment the feasible set are reported mainly at 2060–2310 rpm, exactly where the reproduction does not match the published map (on the interval connected to g = 0 the gain there is about 0.72 of the published one). The demand in [19] is also ambiguous: force K_a g|xdot| or volts g|xdot| compared with 27. The second reading changes the map by a factor of 2–3, and the manuscript does not state its choice. There is also a conceptual issue. Requiring that a specified entry at a = a_lim never saturates the actuator is a conservative but meaningful basin requirement, because it keeps that entry in the linear region. The reformulated condition instead yields checked depths that lie inside the bistable band at 104 of 109 speeds. The comparison of 15.87 mm with 12.75 mm sets a step-entry-checked depth with a 10 % margin, at capped and mostly larger gains, against a margin-free linear limit, and it ignores robustness. Finally, [19] compares clipped DVF with its scheduled gains (its Table 3) and concludes that clipping weakens DVF at high speeds. Section 4.7 implies the opposite: with the clip and g ≥ 1500 the step-entry limit is about 13 mm at 2500 rpm, against a_lim ≈ 6.2 mm at the scheduled gain of about 536 V s/m.  
  *Suggestion:* Overlay the published gain map in Fig. 2a and state the agreement over the whole speed range. State the reading of the demand. Replace 'ill-posed' by 'entry-dependent' unless a mathematical ill-posedness is shown, and restrict the fragmentation claim to the reproduced objective. Add an equal-footing comparison of the published and reformulated designs at 3–4 speeds: same entry, same depths, critical kick or certificate. Discuss explicitly how Section 4.7 relates to the clipped-DVF comparison of [19].
- **M5** (Sections 2.4, 3.2, 4.6, 5.2 and 6.7 (Table 8); abstract and conclusions). Every result hinges on the forced command b(t) and the margin u0 = V_max − b_max, which at the operating points is between a fraction of a volt and about 3 V (e.g. 0.4–2.8 V at 18 of 34 depths at 2500 rpm, Section 6). This margin comes from an idealised force model and is very sensitive to effects left out. (i) Edge (ploughing) forces: at f_z = 0.05 mm the shear term K_tc f_z is 33 N/mm, of the same order as typical edge coefficients for aluminium alloys (about 20–30 N/mm), so the tooth-periodic forcing and b(t) may be substantially larger. (ii) Runout: Table 8 shows that DVF designs that keep 10 % of the voltage free tolerate only 1.1–4.5 µm of eccentric runout before the forced command reaches 9 V. Section 6.7 adds that the designs of Sections 3–5, whose demand reaches exactly 9 V, saturate under any perturbation. Runout of a few micrometres to about 10 µm is common in practice. (iii) Velocity-sensor noise is multiplied by g, and by up to g1 + 2g2 ≈ 11,000 V s/m for the comb. (iv) K_a, K_tc, K_rc and the modal parameters are uncertain. (v) Even the discretisation matters: the 30-layer simulator exceeds the closed-form b_max by up to 8.7 % (about 0.5 V), comparable to u0. As it stands, the gain map, the basin sizes and the certificates may not survive realistic conditions. The DVF runout result, arguably one of the most important practical findings, appears only inside the robustness study of the delayed term.  
  *Suggestion:* Add a sensitivity and uncertainty study of b_max and of the basin. The describing-function estimate of Eq. (23) is cheap enough for this, with certificates at a few points. Cover edge coefficients (e.g. 0–40 N/mm), runout from 0 to 10 µm for DVF, ±10–20 % in K_tc, K_rc, K_a, ζ and k, and a realistic sensor-noise level. Include a runout and noise allowance in the authority condition as a guard on V_max, and show the resulting gain map. Make the certificate robust by replacing b_max with an upper bound over the uncertainty set; Eq. (24) then holds unchanged. Report the DVF runout tolerance in Sections 3–4, the abstract and the conclusions.
- **M6** (Sections 2.2, 4.1, 4.3 and 4.5, Section 7 (Limitations)). Several modelling choices bear directly on attributing the bistability to the clip. (a) The cutting force is linear in chip thickness with no edge term. With a nonlinear (e.g. power-law) force law, the uncontrolled and the unclipped controlled cut can be subcritical near the limit (Kalmár-Nagy et al. 2001; Dombovari et al. 2008; with velocity feedback, Sahu et al. 2021). The statements that the clip 'creates' the bistability and shrinks the basin 52–701 times therefore depend on the linear law. (b) Process damping is neglected, although the case study runs at 25–150 m/min, with chatter wavelengths of a few millimetres at the low speeds where many results are quoted. (c) The single mode has the same modal displacement over the whole axial depth, which is questionable for cuts of 15–40 mm on a flexible workpiece, and the tool is rigid. (d) The actuator is a hard clip at the amplifier input with exact velocity feedback and no loop delay in Sections 3–5. There are no stroke or current limits, no amplifier dynamics, and none of the velocity estimation by integration and filtering used in [19]. (e) In the chatter state the chip is unbounded when the tool plunges and some runs diverge. Clipped-chatter amplitudes of 0.4–0.6 mm, about 8–12 feeds, are far outside the validity of the model. What is established is that the basin of the forced orbit is bounded; a coexisting chatter attractor is not.  
  *Suggestion:* Repeat the key basin computations with a power-law force (exponent 0.7–0.8, matched in mean force) and with edge forces: critical kick versus depth at two speeds, with and without the clip. Discuss process damping and the mode-shape issue. Report the proof-mass stroke reached in the simulations for a plausible proof mass. Restate the bistability result as: the forced orbit has a bounded basin, and outside it the model predicts large-amplitude clipped chatter, bounded or divergent.
- **M7** (Section 6 (Tables 6–8, Figs. 8–10); Section 3.2 (gain cap); Table 1). (a) The cutter diameter is 16 mm. The DVF linear limits reach 42.75 mm (g1 ≤ 3000 V s/m), the DVF + D limits are 29–64 mm (g2 = 2000) and 43–82 mm (g2 = 4000), and the simulated face-entry limits reach 60.8 mm. These depths exceed the flute length of a typical 16 mm end mill (about 2–3 D) and the depths tested in [19] (≤ 10 mm). Much of the 1.78× gain therefore lies where this tool cannot cut and where the single-mode workpiece model is unlikely to hold. (b) The gain caps of 1500 and 3000 V s/m are choices, yet they decide the gain at 157 of 251 speeds (Section 3.4), and the cap is lifted to 20,000 V s/m in Section 6.4. A physical cap should come from the stability margin with loop delay and higher modes and from noise. With the assumed modes, DVF itself tolerates only about 300 µs (Table 8). (c) Making the feedback blind to the tooth-passing harmonics has standard alternatives that are not compared. One is DVF with notch filters at the first few harmonics, cf. generalised notch filters for synchronous rejection in magnetic bearings (Herzog et al., IEEE TCST 4 (1996) 580–586). Another is a band-limited comb with a low-pass Q-filter as in repetitive control. Both would probably keep much of the authority benefit with less of the high-frequency phase lag that causes the delay fragility. (d) The delay-tolerance conclusion (100–200 µs) rests on three assumed second modes (k2 = 6k at 400 and 600 Hz, 40k at 800 Hz, ζ2 = 1 %) evaluated on a coarse delay grid.  
  *Suggestion:* State the flute length and workpiece height of the case study, and restrict the comparisons to feasible depths. Alternatively, present the benefit of the delayed term at a fixed feasible target depth, as the reduction of required gain or the gain in margin and basin at 15–30 mm. Derive the gain cap from a robustness criterion. Add a notch-filtered DVF and/or a band-limited comb, designed by the same authority rule and put through the same robustness tests. Replace the three assumed modes by a parametric map (mode frequency 300–1000 Hz, stiffness ratio, damping) or by an analytical phase-margin bound, on a finer delay grid.
- **M8** (Sections 4.1, 4.4, 4.5 and 4.8; Fig. 4; highlight 3). (a) The lower edge of the bistable band (0.21–0.98 a_lim, median 0.46) is defined by kicks of up to 3 m/s. Such a kick is a 3.7 mm swing, about 74 feeds, far outside the validity of the model (M6) and beyond any realistic disturbance, so the band says little about practical risk. (b) The impulse scale m Δv*/(F_y T) compares an instantaneous kick with the mean, largely static, cutting impulse of a tooth period. It is not obvious which physical disturbance carries 2.4–10.8 % of it. (c) The statement in Section 4.8 that 'a real disturbance of that size, from runout, inhomogeneous material or the tool leaving the workpiece, could do the same' is not supported. Runout is a persistent periodic forcing that shifts the orbit and uses up margin (M5), not a kick, and no realistic disturbance scenario is simulated.  
  *Suggestion:* Replace or complement the band edge in Fig. 4 by iso-contours of the critical kick at bounded disturbance levels in the (n, a) plane, e.g. Δv* = 3, 10 and 30 mm/s. Express kicks also as the equivalent displacement Δv/ω_n (2.3 mm/s ≈ 2.9 µm at 2500 rpm). Compare it with the forced-vibration amplitude (about 3–4 µm in Fig. 3a), the feed and typical runout. Simulate one or two realistic events and show whether they leave the basin: crossing a slot or hole, a step change in radial immersion at a corner, a feed hold and restart in the cut, or a hard inclusion modelled as a short force pulse.
- **M9** (Section 5 (5.2, 5.5, 5.6; Fig. 6; Table 4), Section 7 (second paragraph), Section 6.3). The certificates are rigorous for the collocation models and carefully verified, but their practical value is not argued. (a) The quoted conservatism of 1.5–28 leaves out the 19 of 48 points where no kick up to 3 m/s caused chatter. There the C2 certificates are 1.7–12.5 mm/s, more than 240 times below the simulated basin (more than 1,700 times at 2500 rpm and 0.3 a_lim). (b) The plateau of about 10–13 mm/s at shallow depths in Fig. 6 appears to be set by the validity bound |x − x(t−T)| ≤ 0.5 f_z (δ f_z ω_n ≈ 20 mm/s) rather than by saturation. The paper does not show which constraint limits β at each point: the validity bound, the sector bound or the ellipsoidal shape. (c) Near the limit the describing-function estimate is within 0.88–1.13 of the simulations at almost no cost. The certificate is about 2.3 times conservative there, and a C3 certificate costs 4–10 min and 2–5 GB per point. The step entry is certified only at 0.3 a_lim. (d) With u0 this small, the certificate is hypersensitive to b_max (M5). (e) For DVF + D, the collocated delayed velocity departs from the true one by 45–65 % of g2 Δv after a kick (Section 6.3), so the certificate there is mainly a heuristic that the simulator then checks.  
  *Suggestion:* Add a short subsection on how a practitioner would use the certificate. One option is a basin-aware gain map over the whole speed range that maximises a_lim subject to β ≥ β_req at all depths up to κ a_lim; Table 7 is a first step at four speeds. Compare the certificate explicitly with the describing-function estimate as a design tool. Identify the active constraint, e.g. by re-solving at a few points with δ = 1 and without the validity LMI. Quote the conservatism including the points with no chatter up to 3 m/s. For DVF + D, collocate the velocity history as additional states, or treat xdot(t−T) explicitly, so that the kicked state is represented exactly.
- **M10** (Abstract, highlights, Section 8 (Conclusions), cover letter). Several headline claims need qualifiers. (1) 'Entering from rest lowers the attainable depth ... at 157 of 251 speeds' (highlight 2, abstract, conclusion 2) refers to the step entry, an idealised full engagement at t = 0 that Section 4.8 itself calls a transient 'a real cut need not have'. The realistic face entry reaches a_lim at all 251 speeds, but readers will read the highlight as a general entry penalty. (2) Highlight 3, 'kicks of 2.4–10.8 % of one tooth's impulse can trigger chatter', holds at four speeds, at 0.98 a_lim and in the worst phase, and the physical meaning of the scale is unclear (M8). (3) 'Without the clip no kick below 0.78 m/s does' holds at the 16 tested points only. (4) 'Certified kicks are 1.5 to 28 times below the simulated critical kicks' holds only where the simulated kick is finite (M9). (5) Conclusion 1 reports 'a well-posed gain map and a checked depth of 15.87 mm on average'. But the gain is set by the arbitrary cap at 157 of 251 speeds, and the checked depths lie inside the bistable band at 104 of 109 speeds, so a checked depth is not a safe depth. (6) 'Widens the simulated basin 1.8 to 2.6 times where the DVF certificate almost vanishes' refers to six rule-selected pairs; overall, the delayed design is ahead of the better DVF design at 13 of 22 pairs, with a median ratio of 1.04. (7) 'With an unmodelled second mode' should read 'with an assumed second mode (400–800 Hz)'. (8) Highlight 4 should say 'on collocation models'. (9) The DVF runout tolerance of 1.1–4.5 µm is missing from the abstract and conclusions.  
  *Suggestion:* Rewrite the highlights, abstract, conclusions and cover letter with these qualifiers. For example: 'A step entry (full engagement at t = 0) chatters below 0.97 a_lim at 157 of 251 speeds; a face entry reaches a_lim at all speeds.' Add one sentence on the fragility of the authority margin (runout, noise) and one on the absence of experimental validation.
- **M11** (Section 7 (last paragraph of Limitations), Section 8). The proposed experiment is a single sentence: compare step and face entries at a depth inside the band, and apply kicks with an impact hammer or with the actuator. A literal step entry cannot be realised. Phase-controlled impulsive kicks during cutting are difficult. The outcome is probabilistic because of uncontrolled disturbances, and runout and noise will use up part of the predicted margin. As written, the plan does not show how the central predictions could be falsified.  
  *Suggestion:* Give a concrete validation plan. (i) A hardware-in-the-loop stage in which the real actuator, amplifier and controller act on the identified structure while the regenerative force is emulated in real time (as in Sahu et al. 2021 and 2023, and Mancisidor et al. 2019). This tests the clip-induced bistability safely and allows phase-controlled pulses. (ii) Cutting tests at 2–3 speeds where the model predicts a wide band, with the 'step' realised as a feed hold and restart in the cut or as an entry into a pre-machined pocket, against a face entry. (iii) Perturbations injected as short command pulses through the actuator (within its capacity) or with an instrumented hammer, triggered from the spindle encoder. (iv) Measured runout and forced-command margin. (v) Once-per-tooth sampling for chatter detection, with enough repetitions to estimate probabilities. (vi) The quantities to compare: critical pulse versus depth, and the band edge for bounded perturbations. Also use the published test points of [19] (M3).

- **m9** (Section 3.2 (monotonicity of F_ss in g)). Monotonicity is now checked numerically on 151 gains at all 251 speeds. A simple sufficient argument also exists harmonic by harmonic. The amplitude of harmonic m of b, proportional to |g iω G_cl(iω)|, increases with g if and only if 1 + g Re[iω G_a(iω) G(iω)] > 0. This holds at the tooth-passing harmonics when the mobility iωG is positive real and G_a ≈ K_a, i.e. well above the actuator mode. The only possible sources of non-monotonicity are then the phase alignment in the peak of the sum and the growing depth range.  
  *Suggestion:* Add this argument, or a counterexample, to support the single-interval statement.

## Reviewer B (nonlinear and robust control): major revision

**Summary.** The manuscript takes the single-mode regenerative milling case study of Ozsoy, Sims and Ozturk (2026), in which direct velocity feedback (DVF) drives a proof-mass actuator whose command is clipped at 9 V, and asks what this force bound does to the controlled cut. It first argues that the published authority test depends on the step-entry transient. It replaces it with a steady-state condition, computed from the closed-form forced orbit, plus a time-domain entry check. Using a simulator with full surface memory, the authors show that the clip makes linearly stable cuts bistable. Entering from rest lowers the attainable depth below 0.97 a_lim at 157 of 251 speeds. Near the limit, worst-phase velocity kicks of a few mm/s (2.4–10.8 % of one tooth's cutting impulse) trigger chatter, and a dual-input describing function explains why the basin stays finite at the limit. Ellipsoidal inner estimates of the basin of the forced orbit are then computed with generalised-sector-condition LMIs. Their dead zone is centred on the forced command, giving the level u0 = Vmax − b_max. The certificates are built first on an N-node Chebyshev collocation of the averaged deviation dynamics (C2), then on the time-periodic one (C3), which uses a piecewise-linear periodic Lyapunov matrix and segment-wise levels. Every candidate is re-checked in floating point outside the solver and compared with critical kicks of the same model, critical kicks in the simulator, and off-axis simulations. No violation was found, and the certificates are 1.5–28 times smaller than the simulated critical kicks. Finally, a published delayed-difference (Pyragas-type) term that vanishes on the forced orbit is analysed under the same authority rule. It raises the linear limit (median ×1.78 for g2 = 2000 V s/m) and widens the simulated basin where the DVF certificate is small. However, it barely helps step entries and reduces robustness to loop delay with assumed higher modes, to comb-delay mismatch and to runout. The study is purely computational, and the authors state explicitly that the certificates are statements about the collocation models, not about the delay equation.

**Strengths**

- Scope is stated honestly and repeatedly: the certificates are called theorems about N-node collocation models, not about the delay equation or the simulator; no decay rate is claimed; limitations sections are thorough; and novelty is claimed narrowly, with correct credit to Pyragas, van de Wouw, van Dijk, Li et al., Sahu et al. and Baumann and Elliott.
- The central device is simple, physically meaningful and correct: the dead zone is taken around the forced command b(t), so the authority already spent on the forced vibration lowers the sector level (u0 = Vmax − b_max). I checked both cases of Eq. (22), the S-procedure LMI (23), the inclusion, validity and size LMIs (24)–(26), and the homogeneity argument used to rescale (W, Y, S).
- The periodic certificate is technically sound. It uses a continuous, piecewise-linear periodic W(t) and convexifies (s, s²) in a triangle. aH(t) is enclosed rigorously with a Lipschitz pad. Petersen's lemma is used losslessly for the rank-one scalar uncertainty, the sector levels are set segment by segment, and the bound on the forced command includes the tail beyond 40 harmonics.
- Every solver candidate is re-checked outside the solver. Certificates are compared with the model's own critical kick (a necessary consistency check), with an independent simulator (multiple regeneration, contact loss, periodic coefficient) and with off-axis boundary perturbations. Thousands of runs gave no violation, and the conservatism (1.5–28×) is reported openly.
- The bistability analysis gives a clear mechanism and a useful physical normalisation (kick impulse against one tooth's cutting impulse). It covers the entry map over 251 speeds and basins with and without the clip, and the describing-function argument explains why the basin stays finite at the linear limit.
- Comparisons in Section 6 are fair: the same authority rule for both families, uncapped gains at the bound-limited speeds, 26 DVF candidate gains, and a same-g1 design that isolates the effect of the delayed term. Negative results are reported, namely no gain for step entries at high speed and the robustness costs.
- Numerical settings are documented in detail, and code, stored results and an automated test suite are promised, so the work can be reproduced and audited. A quick re-run of the C2 code shows that the certificate is well converged in N at least at some operating points.

**Major comments**

- **B1** (Sec. 5.1 (last two paragraphs), Sec. 5.7, Sec. 6.3, Sec. 7 (paragraph 'How conservative...'), App. B Table B.9 ('Discretisation')). The paper says clearly that C2/C3 are theorems about the N-node collocation, Eq. (20), not about the delay equation. That is correct and welcome. But the practical meaning of the certificates then depends on how the certified size beta behaves as N grows, and the only evidence given concerns the spectrum: rightmost eigenvalues agree to 3e-8 and a_lim is recovered. Spectral convergence says nothing about convergence of the certified set. I re-ran the authors' code (src/c2_certificate.py, default settings, g = 1500 V s/m, 0.9 a_lim). At 2500 rpm, beta = 3.060, 3.059, 3.058, 3.058, 3.057 and 3.057 mm/s for N = 12, 16, 20 (default), 24, 28 and 32: converged. At 1090 rpm, beta = 6.248, 6.224, 6.196 (default N = 24), 6.171 and 6.160 mm/s for N = 16 to 32: a monotone downward drift of 1.4 % that had not settled. For DVF + D (g1 = 500, g2 = 4000, 2500 rpm, 22 mm), beta = 3.609, 3.577, 3.562 (default) and 3.552 mm/s for N = 12 to 24. These drifts are small and threaten no conclusion, which is good news for the paper. But they go downward, so the default-N values slightly overstate what a finer model would certify, and the reader cannot see any of this.  
  *Suggestion:* Add a short table of beta against N (N, about 1.5N and 2N) at a few representative points: C2 near the limit and at a shallow depth, C3, and DVF + D. State the observed trend, and either report beta at the finest N or state the expected overestimate. Add one sentence in Sec. 5.1 saying that convergence of the eigenvalues does not imply convergence of the certified set, and that this table is the evidence for it.
- **B2** (Sec. 1 (paragraph on the tools for regional guarantees), Sec. 5.1 (paragraph 'The certificates are therefore theorems...'), Sec. 5.7, Sec. 7). The step from the collocation model to the delay equation is set aside with a single citation (Seuret & Gouaisbaut 2014). Yet estimating regions of attraction of linear time-delay systems with saturating inputs, using Lyapunov–Krasovskii functionals combined with (generalised) sector conditions, is an established line of work. Examples are Tarbouriech & Gomes da Silva (IEEE TAC 2000), Cao, Lin & Hu (IEEE TCAS-I 2002), Fridman, Pila & Shaked (IJRNC 2003), Fridman's 2014 monograph, and the discretised Lyapunov–Krasovskii functional of Gu, Kharitonov & Chen (2003). None of these is cited. The averaged deviation dynamics of C2 is exactly their setting: an LTI retarded DDE in four states, one delay, a rank-one delayed term and a scalar saturating input. Moreover, a kick leaves the history deviation at zero at the instant it is applied. For a functional V(phi) = phi(0)'P phi(0) + integral terms, the certified kick is therefore simply (e2' P e2)^(-1/2), with no need to discretise the initial condition. The difficulties are real: T spans 0.65–3.9 structural periods, and the operating point may lie on a lobe that is not connected to T = 0. Low-order Jensen-type functionals will likely fail, so higher-order Bessel–Legendre or discretised complete-type functionals would be needed. The paper should engage with these difficulties rather than state that such a functional 'would be required'.  
  *Suggestion:* Cite the saturated time-delay literature in Secs. 1, 5.1 and 5.7. Attempt a certificate on the averaged delay equation with a Lyapunov–Krasovskii functional and the same sector condition at two or three operating points (e.g., 2500 rpm at 0.5 and 0.9 a_lim). Report the order of functional needed and compare the resulting beta with the collocation beta, or report why the attempt fails. A single success would turn 'theorem about the collocation model' into a statement about the averaged delay equation, and would quantify what collocation contributes.
- **B3** (Sec. 5.2 (Eq. (22) and the paragraph after it; Eqs. (23)–(24)); Sec. 5.4 (segment levels u0,k); Sec. 1, contribution 3; main.tex preamble). Eq. (22) and the LMIs are correct, but the presentation and positioning can be improved. (i) The sharp sector region is asymmetric and time-varying: psi(w,t)[psi(w,t) − v] ≤ 0 holds whenever −(Vmax + b(t)) ≤ w − v ≤ Vmax − b(t). For an ellipsoid centred on the orbit, inclusion in this slab is equivalent to |(K − Gamma)xi| ≤ Vmax − |b(t)| at each t. C2 uses the minimum of this over the period and C3 its minimum over each segment. Stating this makes the gain of C3 at 2500 rpm and 0.25–0.5 a_lim (where |b(t)| peaks only briefly) obvious. It also shows what remains unexploited: non-centred or non-quadratic sets could use the asymmetry. (ii) The 'moving dead zone' is the generalised sector condition applied to a saturation that operates around a known, time-varying input. Reducing the level by the part of the input already used at the operating point is the standard treatment of non-symmetric saturation, non-zero operating points and known exogenous inputs in the monographs the paper cites. It is a sensible and useful adaptation, and contribution 3 should be worded as such. (iii) In Eq. (23), S is not the S-procedure multiplier but its inverse, after the congruence with diag(W, S); the code's own docstring says S = 1/T. (iv) The result is spread over several paragraphs. The preamble defines \newtheorem{proposition}, but no proposition is ever stated.  
  *Suggestion:* State a lemma with the sharp asymmetric condition, and give Eq. (22) as its time-uniform corollary. State C2 and C3 as Propositions with explicit assumptions: a bound b_bar ≥ sup_t |b(t)| with b_bar < Vmax, and strict LMIs at the listed points. Give a short proof: sector lemma, S-procedure, congruence, strict decrease and forward invariance, then exponential convergence. Say explicitly that S is the inverse multiplier. Reword contribution 3 as an adaptation, citing where the offset/non-symmetric saturation reduction appears in the monographs already cited.
- **B4** (Sec. 5.2, Eq. (25) and the paragraph after it; Sec. 5.7; Fig. 6 (depths 0.15–0.3 a_lim); Sec. 7, paragraph 2). (i) With h_j = f_z sin(phi) − dx cos(phi), the bound |dx| ≤ delta f_z guarantees a positive chip only for phi < pi − arctan(delta), i.e. about 153.4 deg for delta = 0.5. Inside the certified set the chip can therefore vanish over the last ~27 deg of the 90 deg engagement arc. By my calculation with Krc/Ktc = 0.273, that arc carries about 30 % of the integral that defines alpha_yy in Eq. (9). 'Excludes only gross loss of contact' is accurate but understates how much of the regenerative coefficient can switch off inside the set. The certified model thus omits a nonlinearity that is active there, and Sec. 5.2 itself reports that, without the bound, states inside the ellipsoid ended in chatter in the simulator. (ii) delta is a free choice, and it drives the certificate at shallow depths. Re-running the authors' code with delta = 1 instead of 0.5, beta rises from 11.7 to 18.5 mm/s (+58 %) at 1090 rpm and 0.15 a_lim, and from 8.74 to 10.0 mm/s (+15 %) at 2870 rpm and 0.3 a_lim, but only from 3.058 to 3.071 mm/s at 2500 rpm and 0.9 a_lim. The flat certificates at shallow depths in Fig. 6 therefore partly reflect delta, not saturation. This matters for the claim in Sec. 7 about where the conservatism comes from.  
  *Suggestion:* Quantify point (i) in Sec. 5.2. Report beta for delta in {0.25, 0.5, 1, no bound} at one shallow and one near-limit depth, and assign the shallow-depth plateau to its causes. Justify delta physically, or remove it. A principled way to remove delta is to model contact loss itself as a second sector-bounded nonlinearity. Per angular bin, max{h_s + y, 0} − h_s lies in the sector [0, 1] with respect to y, and it satisfies a local generalised sector condition of level h_s(phi) wherever h_s > 0. This fits the same LMI machinery with extra multipliers. At least discuss this route in Secs. 5.7 and 7.
- **B5** (Sec. 5.3, items (ii)–(iv); App. B ('Independent check', 'Enclosure and command bound', 'Settings and cost'); Sec. 5.2, last paragraph; Sec. 6.6 ('corrected for the command bound')). The solver-free acceptance test is one of the best features of the paper, but words such as 'proved', 'theorem' and 'guarantee' overstate its rigour. (a) Positive definiteness is tested in floating point against a tolerance of 1e3 (n+1) eps_mach times max|lambda|, and App. B concedes that this is not an interval-arithmetic proof. (b) For C2 the test is made on matrices formed in re-centred coordinates: Tm^-1 A Tm, with Tm built from Cholesky factors of W whose condition numbers reach 1e10. Unlike for C3, the tolerance does not include the rounding made when forming Tm^-1 A Tm and A W (see check() in src/c2_certificate.py). (c) The relative margins lambda_min/||M|| of accepted candidates are not reported, although the one C3 failure (2500 rpm, 0.98 a_lim) is precisely a margin below this round-off bound. (d) The C2 command level was first computed with a bound that was not rigorous, and then corrected after the fact by rescaling (W, Y, S). The rescaling is valid: the decrease LMI is homogeneous, the inclusion scales exactly, and the validity and size bounds are preserved for a factor c ≤ 1. But the paragraph reads like an erratum, and the corrected numbers are scattered over Secs. 5 and 6.  
  *Suggestion:* Make the final acceptance test rigorous. For example, factor M − alpha I by Cholesky in floating point, with alpha taken from Rump's error bound (BIT 46 (2006) 433–452), or use interval arithmetic. Apply it to the stored (A, W, Y, S) in the original coordinates; the matrices have order ≤ 37 and there are a few hundred of them, so this is cheap. 'Proved for the N-node model' would then be justified; otherwise write 'numerically verified' throughout. Report the achieved margins, and for C3 the vertex where the minimum occurs. Recompute C2 directly with the rigorous bound of App. B and drop the correction narrative. Use a distinct symbol for the rigorous bound (e.g., b_bar), because Sec. 2.4 defines b_max as a sampled peak.
- **B6** (Sec. 4.7 (first paragraph); Secs. 5.5–5.6; Sec. 7 (first paragraph, last sentence); Sec. 8, item 3). The certificates bound the set of initial deviations from the orbit, i.e. an instantaneous kick or an arbitrary deviation at one phase. Sec. 4.7, however, names runout, inhomogeneous material and the tool leaving the workpiece as the disturbances that could trigger chatter. Sec. 7 then says that the certificates 'bound from below ... the disturbance that the remaining authority can absorb'. Those disturbances are persistent or repeated, and an invariant ellipsoid of the unforced deviation dynamics does not cover them. The certified sizes are also small in physical terms. Near the limit beta is about 1–5 mm/s, i.e. a vibration amplitude dv/omega_n of about 1.2–6 µm at 129 Hz. That is the same order as the runout (1.1–4.5 µm) that Sec. 6.8 finds sufficient to drive the forced command to the 9 V clip. An MSSP reader needs exactly this interpretation, and Section 5 does not give it.  
  *Suggestion:* Convert certified kicks into displacement amplitudes and impulses, as Sec. 4.4 does for dv*, and compare them with realistic disturbance levels. Either restrict the wording in Secs. 4.7, 7 and 8 to instantaneous perturbations, or add a disturbance-tolerance analysis with the same LMIs. That analysis would find the largest L2- or amplitude-bounded force disturbance that keeps the state inside E(P) (e.g., Hu & Lin 2001; Fang, Lin & Hu, Automatica 40 (2004)). It would link the certificates to runout and material variation.
- **B7** (Sec. 6.3 ('Certificate' paragraph); Sec. 6.6; Table 7; Fig. 9; Sec. 6.7). In the DVF + D model, xdot(t − T) is taken as the derivative of the Chebyshev interpolant at theta = −T. After a kick, the model's delayed command departs from that of the delay equation by 0.45–0.65 g2*dv over the first two tooth periods, about half the delayed term itself, against about 0.1 % for DVF. Table 7 and Fig. 9 therefore compare certificates whose models differ greatly in fidelity for exactly the perturbation being certified. How this error and beta behave with N is not reported; in my run, beta drifts from 3.61 to 3.55 mm/s for N = 12 to 24 at 2500 rpm, 22 mm, (g1, g2) = (500, 4000). The evidence that does support the DVF + D model is that its critical kicks agree with the simulator. From the stored validation files, the ratio of model to simulated critical kick is 0.80–1.01 (median 0.98) for the map designs and 0.79–1.00 (median 0.96) for the same-g1 designs, no worse than for DVF (0.66–1.02). The text says only that the certificate 'rests more on the simulator check'.  
  *Suggestion:* Report this agreement explicitly, together with the size of the d_N error and beta as a function of N. Consider a state representation in which the delayed velocity is itself a state rather than the derivative of an interpolant. Options are collocating the history of xdot (or of the command) as well, or using a piecewise-polynomial or semi-discretised history that represents the kink. In Table 7, Fig. 9 and the text, call the Section 6 certificates certificates of the collocation model, and add this caveat wherever the 'certified depths' of DVF and DVF + D are compared.
- **B8** (Sec. 6.8 (all paragraphs); Table 8; Fig. 10c,f; Sec. 6.9; Sec. 7 (paragraph 3); Abstract (last sentence); Sec. 1 (contribution 4); also the cover letter). The headline delay tolerances (DVF 300 µs; g2 = 1000: 200 µs; g2 = 2000–4000: 100 µs) have several limits. (a) They are read off the grid {0, 50, 100, 200, 300, 400 µs} with an arbitrary criterion, a_lim(tau) ≥ 0.5 a_lim(0), so the families differ by a single grid step. (b) They depend on hypothetical second modes (k2 = 6k at 400 and 600 Hz, 40k at 800 Hz, zeta2 = 1 %). The abstract and Sec. 1 call these modes 'unmodelled', which suggests a generic result. (c) The analysis is purely linear: the effect of loop delay and higher modes on the basin of the clipped loop, the paper's main subject, is not examined, although the simulator supports a loop delay. (d) Implementation effects that bear directly on 'authority preservation' are not discussed: T as an integer number of samples or a fractional delay, the zero-order hold, spindle-speed fluctuation, and noise amplified by |K| up to g1 + 2g2. (e) E(i omega) is a phase lead in the lower half and a lag in the upper half of each band between tooth-passing harmonics, so 'adds phase lag' (Sec. 7) needs qualifying. Finally, the standard repetitive-control remedy for exactly this fragility, a low-pass Q-filter in the delay path, E_Q(s) = 1 − Q(s)e^(−sT) (Hara et al., IEEE TAC 33 (1988)), is not discussed. Nor is a notch at the dominant tooth harmonic(s) in the DVF path, which would remove most of b(t) where the first harmonic dominates (e.g., 2500 rpm).  
  *Suggestion:* Compute continuous delay margins by bisection in tau, both for the uncut loop and for a_lim(tau)/a_lim(0) = 0.9 and 0.5. Show how they move with the assumed modal frequency, stiffness ratio and damping, or present them explicitly as one illustrative scenario. Write 'assumed' rather than 'unmodelled' in the abstract, Sec. 1 and the cover letter. Add one nonlinear check: the simulated critical kick with tau = 100–200 µs at two operating points. Discuss, and ideally evaluate, a Q-filtered comb and a notch-filtered DVF as baselines.
- **B9** (Abstract; Highlights; Sec. 6.7 (paragraph beginning 'Where the DVF certificate almost vanishes'); Sec. 7 (paragraphs 1–3); Sec. 8, items 3–4). Several summary statements are stronger or more selective than the results. (a) The abstract gives the median ×1.78 rise of the linear limit, but not that the step-entry limit rises by a median of only ×1.03 and that the bistable band widens (Sec. 6.5). Given the paper's own thesis that a_lim is not the attainable depth, this omission matters. (b) 'Widens the simulated basin 1.8 to 2.6 times' refers to 6 of 22 pairs selected by a threshold on the DVF certificate of 2.1 mm/s; the next pair, at 2.6 mm/s, gives ×1.45. Over all 22 same-budget pairs the median is ×1.14 (larger at 19 of 22), and ×1.45 at depths of 18 mm and more. 'A rule fixed before the simulations were compared' cannot be verified by a reader. (c) 'Certify a basin' is too strong: the LMIs certify an ellipsoidal inner estimate of the basin of the collocation model. (d) In Sec. 7, 'This gap does not come from the model' is contradicted by the next sentence. The model's critical kick is 0.62–0.99 of the simulated one, so the model accounts for up to a factor of 1.6 at some points; Sec. 5.5 correctly says 'mainly'. (e) See B6 on 'the disturbance that the remaining authority can absorb'.  
  *Suggestion:* Add the step-entry result to the abstract and to Sec. 8, item 4. Report the all-pairs statistic next to the selected subset, and justify the 2.1 mm/s threshold physically rather than procedurally. Write 'certify an ellipsoidal inner estimate of the basin ... on collocation models' and 'the gap lies mainly in the certificate'. Adjust the highlights to match ('certify basins' becomes 'certify inner estimates of the basin').
- **B10** (Whole manuscript; in particular Secs. 3–6, App. B ('Settings and cost'), Sec. 4.1 (preliminary runs), Secs. 5.2 and 5.5 (development runs), Fig. 7). - **m3** (Sec. 5.2 (after Eq. (26)); Sec. 5.7; App. B ('Conditions of C2/C3')). 'No decay rate is claimed' is unnecessarily weak. Strict LMIs at finitely many points imply exponential decay at some rate, and the guaranteed rate is the largest lambda for which the checked matrices remain positive definite; it can be computed cheaply from the verified candidate. Conversely, the solve imposes lambda = 0.05 rho, which slightly lowers beta compared with lambda close to 0.  
  *Suggestion:* Report the verified decay rate, which tells a practitioner how fast a certified perturbation dies out. Quantify the effect of the imposed lambda on beta with one final solve at a much smaller lambda.
- **m4** (App. B, 'Model runs used for comparison', last sentence; Table 4, row 'Off-axis runs ending in chatter'; Sec. 5.6). The 3384 off-axis runs for C3 used only the last four remembered passes. Sec. 4.1 and App. A show that four passes are inadequate once chatter develops: runs diverge spuriously, and one amplitude is 27 % too large. Because the off-axis test asks exactly whether chatter develops, it should use the protocol validated in App. A.  
  *Suggestion:* Rerun the C3 off-axis tests (or a representative subset) with the full surface memory, or explain why the outcome cannot change.
- **m5** (Sec. 5.6, paragraphs 2 and 5; Table 4). C3 grows by 3–12 % from M = 16 to M = 32 segments, while its median advantage over C2 is 6 %. The point at 2500 rpm and 0.98 a_lim fails at both values of M. The decomposition shows that the gain comes from the phase-dependent level, which the averaged model alone already delivers (×1.06–1.15) at much lower cost, while the periodic coefficient costs 2–6 %.  
  *Suggestion:* Say which certificate a practitioner should use. Report C3 at M = 32, or an extrapolated value, wherever it is compared with C2. Explain the failure at 2500 rpm and 0.98 a_lim (conditioning) and whether a rigorous check (B5) would accept a candidate there.
- **m6** (Sec. 5.6, last paragraph; Table 5). The counterexample is at g = 0 (no control), outside the setting of the paper. It illustrates a known fact: averaging fails for straight teeth and interrupted cutting, which the paper itself credits to Davies, Bayly and Merdol & Altintas.  
  *Suggestion:* Shorten it to two sentences or move it to Supplementary Material.
- **m7** (App. B (table numbered 'B.9'); Fig. 7 (legend and panel a); main.tex front and back matter). The appendix table is numbered B.9 because the table counter is not reset in the appendix. The legend of Fig. 7 uses '(C1)', which is never defined in the text. Fig. 7a uses unevenly spaced depth bins, with 0.95 and 0.98 squeezed together, without explanation. - **m8** (Sec. 3 (title and last paragraph of Sec. 3.3); Abstract; Sec. 1, contribution 1). 'Well-posed' is used informally. The meaning intended is a criterion in closed form, independent of the entry transient, whose feasible set was a single interval on the gain grid. In mathematics the term means existence, uniqueness and continuous dependence. The single-interval property is an observation on a 151-point gain grid, not a proof.  
  *Suggestion:* Use 'well-defined', or define the term, and say that the single-interval property was observed on the grid.
- **m9** (Sec. 5.3; App. B ('Settings and cost'); Table 4). Clarabel 'stops short of the optimum', and several stored C2 solutions have status AlmostSolved (e.g., 2500 rpm at 0.3, 0.4, 0.95 and 0.98 a_lim). Validity is unaffected because the check is independent. But the reported conservatism (1.5–28×) then mixes the conservatism of the method with the suboptimality of the solver.  
  *Suggestion:* Report for each certificate the solver's dual objective, which bounds the optimal beta of the LMI problem from above, or the relative gap. The reader can then see how much of the gap belongs to the method.
- **m10** (Table 4; Sec. 5.5; App. B ('Model runs used for comparison')). Table 4 mixes three kinds of evidence: necessary consistency checks (beta below the model's own critical kick), validation against the simulator, and sampled checks that the paper itself calls weak. The model critical kicks in the C2 and C3 columns also follow different protocols: minimum over 4 phases with about 2 % bisection resolution, against 16 phases with 0.06 % resolution.  
  *Suggestion:* Split the table into 'consistency with the model' and 'validation in the simulator', and use the same protocol for the model kicks in both columns.
- **m11** (Sec. 6.1, item (ii); Sec. 6.3 ('Closed form')). That K(s) is positive real gives passivity only with an ideal collocated force actuator. With the inertial actuator, stability of the loop without cutting relies on E(i omega) being approximately i omega T near the 8.4 Hz actuator resonance, so the D term adds little gain there; this is worth one sentence. Counting zeros in the right half-plane 'along the imaginary axis up to 5 kHz' requires an argument that no crossing occurs above 5 kHz.  
  *Suggestion:* Add the sentence about stability of the loop without cutting with the inertial actuator. State the high-frequency bound used, e.g. that the loop term decays like 1/|s| so that |Delta(i omega) − 1| < 1 beyond 5 kHz.
- **m12** (Sec. 7, paragraph 2 ('Lur'e-type or sum-of-squares Lyapunov functions ... are the natural next steps')). The suggested ways of reducing conservatism are given without references, and the attribution of the gap to 'the ellipsoidal shape ... the sector bound ... and the validity bound' is not quantified. B4 shows that the validity bound matters mainly at shallow depths. Relevant references are Hu, Huang & Lin (IEEE TAC 2004) on Lur'e functions with generalised sector conditions; Hu, Teel & Zaccarian (IEEE TAC 2006) on quadratic and non-quadratic Lyapunov functions for saturated systems; Dai, Hu, Teel & Zaccarian (SCL 2009) on piecewise-quadratic functions for dead zones; and Papachristodoulou, Peet & Lall (IEEE TAC 2009) on sum-of-squares methods for time-delay systems.  
  *Suggestion:* Cite these works. Support, or rephrase as a hypothesis, the statement about where the gap comes from, e.g. by evaluating one richer certificate (a Lur'e–Postnikov function or several multipliers) at a few points, and runs with no validity bound.
- **m13** (Sec. 5.5 (paragraph 'A kick probes one of 4+N directions ...')). The off-axis test uses 18 fixed boundary points (both signs of the points that maximise six coordinates, plus six random ones) at four phases. The paper itself notes that the sampled check is weak, since ellipsoids 2.5–3 times too large passed it. A test that searches for the worst direction would be much stronger.  
  *Suggestion:* Add a falsification search: optimise the boundary direction and phase to maximise growth in the model with the true dead zone (for example, starting from the most amplified directions of the linearisation with the command saturated), and simulate the worst points found.
- **m14** (Sec. 4.5, Eqs. (17)–(18)). The critical amplitude A* is the harmonic-balance prediction of an unstable limit cycle that separates the basins. Using it as a basin boundary rests on the filtering hypothesis, on chatter frequencies incommensurate with the tooth frequency (Eq. 17), and on an empirically fitted, phase-dependent factor p. The agreement of 0.61–1.13 is good, but these caveats should be stated.  
  *Suggestion:* Add one or two sentences on these standard caveats, and say how strongly p depends on the kick phase.
